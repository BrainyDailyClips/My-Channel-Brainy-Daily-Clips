# Anaphora / Direct-Address Style

A permission-giving, sustained-direct-address style built entirely on repeated sentence
openers and parallel structure. No named third-party examples, no borrowed authority — the
entire persuasive weight comes from rhythm and direct address to "you." This is the lowest-
fabrication-risk mode in this skill because it structurally avoids the failure mode of citing
real people or inventing proof stories.

## Why it works for retention

- **Permission, not accusation.** The viewer isn't told what's wrong with them — they're told
  what they're allowed to stop doing. "You do not have to explain yourself" lands as relief,
  not criticism. Relief keeps people watching; shame makes people click away.
- **Sustained "you."** Every sentence is addressed directly to the viewer, with no narrator
  distance ("some people..." / "I once knew a man who..."). This is more intimate per line than
  the proof-story-driven modes, which is what makes it work at length without needing new
  "plot" — the intimacy itself carries 30-45 minutes.
- **Anaphora runs train anticipation.** Once a listener hears "You do not have to X" twice,
  their brain predicts the third one before it arrives — and getting the prediction confirmed
  is mildly pleasurable. This is a known rhetorical device (preachers, MLK's "I have a dream,"
  self-help audio) — recognize it, don't treat it as discovered-on-the-fly.
- **Closing triads resolve the tension the run built.** Three short parallel statements at the
  end of a chapter give the ear a sense of completion before moving on.

## Core mechanics

1. **Permission-giving tone throughout.** Frame every instruction as something the listener is
   *allowed* to do or stop doing, not something they're failing to do. "You don't have to
   perform peace you don't feel" — not "stop performing peace you don't feel."

2. **Sustained direct address — no third parties.** Do not introduce named examples, proof
   stories, or "a woman I knew once" style anecdotes. The style works specifically because it
   never leaves the second person. Breaking into a third-person story is a tonal shift this mode
   should avoid unless the user explicitly asks for a hybrid.

3. **Anaphora run construction.** Build runs of 3+ consecutive sentences sharing the same
   opener, each completing it with a different, specific continuation:
   - "You do not have to explain your boundaries. You do not have to justify your peace. You do
     not have to perform forgiveness you haven't finished feeling."
   Vary openers chapter to chapter ("You do not have to...", "You are allowed to...", "Nobody
   gets to...", "It is not your job to...") so the whole script doesn't flatten into one phrase.

4. **Closing triads.** End each chapter with three short, parallel reversals or summations — the
   rhythmic "button" on the section:
   - "Not because you stopped caring. Not because you got cold. Because you finally got clear."

5. **Chapter-based pacing for long-form targets.** Build in chapters of roughly 300-500 words
   each. For a 30-45 minute target at 130-150 wpm, that's ~6,000-7,000 total words across
   roughly 12-18 chapters. State the word count and estimated runtime (at both 130 and 150 wpm)
   before presenting a full draft — don't let the user discover the length mismatch after
   reading. If building toward a long target, it's fine to deliver in stages (a sample chapter,
   then confirm direction, then the rest) rather than attempting the full length in one pass.

## Style-specific self-audit additions

On top of the standard audit (anchoring, phrase-overlap, false-attribution), check:
- **New sub-idea per chapter, not padding.** Each chapter's anaphora run should introduce a
  genuinely different angle on the topic, not restate the previous chapter's point in new
  words. A listener should be able to summarize what each chapter added.
- **Tone drift.** Permission-giving tone can slide into accusatory tone over a long script
  without the writer noticing ("you do not have to..." sliding into "you need to stop...").
  Flag and fix any sentence that reads as blame rather than release.
