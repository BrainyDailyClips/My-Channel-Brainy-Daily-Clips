# Voice Checklist

Structure gets a script organized. This is what makes it sound like an actual person and not
an AI imitation of one. Extract these five things per source transcript — don't skip straight
to "make it sound raw," that produces generic, tic-parody output instead of a real voice match.

## 1. Named filler/transition words
Every speaker has 2-4 specific words or phrases they lean on as connective tissue. Write down
the actual words, not a category. Examples pulled from real sources:
- "right?" as a check-in after a claim
- "and so" as the default transition into a consequence
- "here's the thing" as a reset phrase before a bigger idea
- "like" as a soft qualifier before a comparison

Use the source's actual words. Don't substitute a generic filler if the source didn't use one.

## 2. Self-interruption / mid-sentence restart
Real spoken delivery frequently catches itself and re-phrases mid-thought instead of
delivering one clean sentence. Example: "I get it because it's just like everyone's like, 'oh
believe in yourself.'" That's a visible reroute, not a clean linear sentence. Written scripts
default to clean sentences — deliberately break a few per script to counter this.

## 3. Direct rhetorical questions to the viewer
Count how often the source turns and asks the audience something directly ("Have you ever felt
that way before?"). This creates a pause and makes the monologue feel like a conversation. Aim
to place at least 1-2 per script, ideally right after a proof story, where the audience is most
likely to self-identify with what was just described.

## 4. In-line repetition for emphasis
Different from repeating a theme across sections — this is repeating a short phrase back-to-
back within the same breath for rhythm: "check, check, check" / "confidence is like a muscle...
work out that muscle." Sprinkle 2-3 of these per script at your highest-stakes moments (thesis
lines, list closers).

## 5. Specificity over intensifiers
This is the highest-leverage item on this list. Replace every vague amount, duration, or
feeling-word with something concrete and checkable:
- "a long time" → "three weeks" / "four days straight"
- "a lot of money" → "$5,000" / "$1,400 a month"
- "really early" → "4:00 a.m."
- "someone I know" → "my sister" / "a coworker"
Concrete numbers and named specifics are what make a story feel lived-in rather than invented.

## Calibrating intensity to format
- **Short hook-only clips (15-30 sec):** lean harder into fragmentation and repetition — it's
  a scroll-stopping burst, not a sustained conversation.
- **Full-length videos (3+ min):** favor the flowing, specific style over heavy fragmentation.
  Constant stammering across a whole video reads as a performed tic rather than genuine
  spontaneity, and hurts retention instead of helping it.

## Self-check before finalizing
Read the draft out loud. If it sounds like a good writer doing a voiceover — competent, clean,
but clearly pre-written — that's a signal these five items weren't applied explicitly enough.
Go back through this list line by line rather than doing another general "make it more human"
pass.
