# 5 Signs Someone Is Jealous Of You

- **Mode:** viral-script-engineer, action-first / raw-human voice, **secular register**
- **Angle (new mechanism):** every sign shows up in how they react to **your good news**, in the first 60 seconds
- **Length:** ~1,600 words, about 10:45 at 150 wpm / 12:20 at 130 wpm
- **List order:** least expected first, most validating/heaviest last (escalating stakes)

---

## Full script

### 1. Contradiction hook (0:00)

Tell somebody your good news. Then watch the next sixty seconds. Not their words. Their face. Their next sentence. Where the conversation goes. Because jealousy almost never says "I'm jealous." It says "congrats"... and then it changes the subject. I'm about to give you five signs someone is jealous of you, and every single one of them shows up in that first minute. And number five? Number five is the one nobody wants to hear.

### 2. Brief vulnerability (0:30)

I learned this the embarrassing way. I got a raise. Eleven percent, my first one in three years. And I called the person I'd known the longest. Eleven years. And the first thing... I still remember this... the first thing out of their mouth was, "Must be nice." Two words. I laughed it off. I shouldn't have.

### 3. Promise stack (0:50)

So if you've ever walked out of a conversation feeling smaller and couldn't explain why. If you've stopped telling somebody your wins and you don't even remember deciding to stop. If you keep giving one person the benefit of the doubt and it keeps costing you. This is for you.

### 4. Debunk the wrong answer (1:05)

Here's the thing. Most people look for jealousy in the wrong place. They look for the obvious stuff: the eye roll, the gossip, the shady comment under your post. And listen, that stuff is real. But somebody who's been in your life for five, ten years is not going to roll their eyes at you. They've had too much practice. They know all the right words. So stop grading the words. Watch the reaction. Specifically, the reaction to your good news.

### 5. Core thesis (1:30)

How someone handles your bad news tells you if they're kind. How they handle your good news tells you if they're *for* you.

I'm going to say that one more time. Bad news shows you kindness. Good news shows you loyalty.

### 7. Universalizing principle + real citation (1:45)

And this isn't just a gut feeling. A psychologist named Shelly Gable has spent years studying exactly this: what people do when you tell them something good happened. Her team sorted the reactions into four types. Some people light up and ask questions. Some people say "that's nice" and move on. Some people go looking for the problem in it. And some people just ignore it and talk about something else. Only the first one actually builds the relationship.

And here's what got me. In the couples they studied, how a partner responded to good news said *more* about the relationship than how they responded to bad news. Did you catch that? Anybody can show up for a funeral. Not everybody can show up for your promotion.

### 8.5. The five signs

**Sign number one.** They say congratulations, and that's it.

"Congrats." "That's awesome." "Good for you." And then... nothing. No "how'd you pull that off?" No "when do you start?" No "what's the salary?" Okay, maybe don't ask that one. Now, not everybody is excitable. Real talk, some of the most loyal people you know are quiet. But quiet, loyal people still ask questions. Because a question means they're paying attention, and paying attention to your win costs a jealous person something. Every follow-up question is ten more seconds of staring at what you have and they don't. So they keep it short. Clean. Polite. Done.

You call it polite. A psychologist calls it passive-constructive: technically positive, actually empty. Have you ever gotten a "congrats" that felt colder than silence? Yeah. That's the one.

**Sign number two.** And this is where it starts getting uncomfortable.

You share your news, and within about three sentences the conversation is about them. "Oh, you got the apartment? Man, I've been looking for eight months, the market is insane, my landlord just raised my rent two hundred dollars..." And now *you're* comforting *them*. Are you seeing this? You walked in with good news and you walked out as their therapist.

There's a sociologist named Charles Derber who wrote about this back in 1979. He called it the *shift response*: you start a conversation, and someone quietly shifts it back to themselves. Everybody does it sometimes. I do it. You do it. But a jealous person does it every single time it's your win. So watch the pattern, not the moment. One pivot is a habit. A pivot every time you have good news? That's a strategy.

**Sign number three.** Oh, this one is sneaky.

They answer your good news with a warning. You say you're starting a business: "Well, you know most small businesses fail, right?" You say you're moving: "That city's so expensive, though." You say you're engaged: "Wow. Fast." And it always comes dressed up as concern. "I'm just looking out for you." "Somebody has to be realistic."

Now listen, real friends *will* warn you. That's part of the job. But a real friend warns you *after* they celebrate you. A jealous person skips the celebration and goes straight to the warning. You call it concern. A psychologist calls it active-destructive responding, and in that same research, the destructive reactions went hand in hand with worse relationships. They're not protecting you from the risk. They're shrinking your win back down to a size they can live with.

**Sign number four.** And this one is going to explain a lot.

They're genuinely happy for you, about *some* things. You post vacation pictures, they hype you up. You get a new dog, they're first in the comments. But the day you win in *their* lane (their career, their dream, the thing they've been trying to do for years) they go cold. Have you noticed that?

A psychologist named Abraham Tesser built a whole model around this back in the 1980s. Short version: when someone close to you outperforms you at something that's central to who you are, it doesn't feel like their win. It feels like your loss. Close plus relevant equals threat. So the friend who's been trying to write a book for six years can celebrate your wedding all day long. But the day *you* sign a book deal? Different person. It's not that they don't love you. It's that your win is standing right on top of their wound.

And here's the part nobody tells you. The closer they are, the harder it hits. Which means the people most likely to be jealous of you aren't strangers. They're the ones who have your number saved.

**Sign number five.** I told you this one was heavy.

They're always there when you're losing, and they disappear when you're winning. When you got laid off, they called you every day. When you went through the breakup, they showed up with food. You'd swear this is the most loyal person in your life. And in those moments? Maybe they were.

But then things turn around. You get the job. You get the place. You get *happy*. And suddenly they're busy. Texts take two days. Plans keep falling through. Here's what's going on underneath. Some people only feel close to you when they're above you. Your low point let them be the strong one, the stable one, the one with their life together. Your comeback takes that away.

So they don't leave when you're down. They leave when you get up. Let me say that again. They don't leave when you're down. They leave when you get up. And you'll spend months wondering what you did wrong. You didn't do anything wrong. You just stopped needing to be rescued.

### 9. Concrete small-step tactic: the 60-second test (8:00)

So what do you actually do with this? Not a confrontation. Please don't text anybody "I know you're jealous of me." That never goes the way you think it will.

Here's what you do instead. Next time something good happens, keep it small. A good review at work. You finally ran three miles without stopping. You paid off a credit card. Tell it to the one person you're unsure about. Then stop talking, and watch the next sixty seconds. Do they ask a question? Do they pivot? Do they warn you? Do they go flat?

Do it once and it's a data point. Do it three times over a couple of months and it's a pattern. And once you see the pattern, you don't have to cut anybody off. You just change what you share. Your wins go to the people who ask follow-up questions. Everybody else gets the weather.

### 10. Preempt the objection (9:00)

But I know what you're thinking. What if I'm wrong? What if they're just having a bad week? Maybe they are. That's exactly why you don't decide off one conversation. One cold reaction is a bad day. Three is information. And even if you're wrong about *why* they do it, the effect on you is the same. You still walk away smaller. You're allowed to protect yourself from that.

### 11. Second thesis (9:25)

Jealousy doesn't hide in what people say *about* you. It hides in what they can't say *to* you. "I'm proud of you." Four words. Watch who can't get them out.

### 12. Physical proof + metaphor (9:40)

And here's how you'll know you're with the right people. Pay attention to your body after you share good news. With the wrong people, your shoulders come up. You start downplaying it halfway through the sentence: "It's not a big deal, it's just..." You shrink it before they get the chance to. With the right people? You talk faster. You get louder. You forget to be modest. That's not ego. That's safety.

Your good news is a flashlight. It doesn't make anybody jealous. It just shows you who already was.

### 13. CTA loop (10:10)

And if one of these signs had a face attached to it while you were listening, watch the video on screen right now. I break down exactly what they mean when they hit you with "must be nice," and how to answer it so they never say it again.
